IMPLEMENTATION CASE STUDY AND EVALUATION FRAMEWORK · MANUSCRIPT

AI-Enabled Enterprise-Scale Digital Health Infrastructure

A descriptive case study of a provincial AI-enabled HMIS and a national-to-global evaluation framework that separates reported implementation scale from verified effectiveness.

ResearchAbid Ghaffar and Muhammad Arfan

Aim

To characterise the architecture and AI-enabled operating model of a multi-hospital Healthcare Management Information System (HMIS) and to establish a defensible framework for measuring its impact — deliberately separating reported implementation scale from verified effectiveness.

The paper describes a descriptive, implementation-focused case study. It is a system characterisation and evaluation protocol, not a controlled effectiveness study.

Research questions

  1. How is the HMIS organised to support longitudinal patient information and cross-facility operations?
  2. Which AI-enabled capabilities are embedded in or connected to clinical and administrative workflows?
  3. Through what mechanisms could these capabilities improve information availability, workflow efficiency, patient safety and population-health intelligence?
  4. Which quantitative and qualitative measures are needed to determine whether the system produces meaningful benefits without unacceptable harms?
  5. What governance and interoperability conditions are necessary for safe and equitable operation at scale?

Architecture

A federated three-tier ecosystem: health facilities (Tier 1), district data hubs (Tier 2) and a secure provincial core (Tier 3) hosting a central longitudinal patient record, shared services, embedded AI, logistics, workforce management and the patient mobile-app server — mediated by an interoperability hub and secure gateway. Named standards include HL7/FHIR, ICD-11, SNOMED CT, LOINC, RxNorm, ICHI and ICPC-2; the paper notes that naming a standard does not establish conformance.

AI-enabled capabilities and the evidence they require

Clinical documentation support

Speech dictation and note assistance; clinicians review and authenticate the final record.

Longitudinal record retrieval

Consolidated access to prior notes, allergies, medicines, tests and images.

Diagnostic imaging support

AI-assisted prioritisation or interpretation support; final interpretation remains with a qualified professional.

Medication safety

Configured drug, allergy, dose and prescription checks flag risks for pharmacist or prescriber review.

Preventive screening

Eligible-patient identification and screening workflows with clinical confirmation.

Disease surveillance

ICD-11-based disease capture and anomaly signals routed to authorised administrators for verification.

AI is positioned as decision support under human oversight. Each capability is paired with the evidence needed to substantiate its value, such as time-motion studies, alert precision and override rates, or model sensitivity and specificity with subgroup performance.

Theory of change and evaluation

Structured longitudinal records and connected workflows provide the information substrate; AI functions may reduce information-processing burdens or prioritise review; and these changes may contribute to patient, operational and population-health outcomes — but only if the functions are accurate, adopted, integrated and governed. Expected benefits are expressed as testable hypotheses at patient, facility and population level, with an evaluation design covering technical performance, adoption, usability, clinical processes, patient experience, equity, safety and economics.

The paper treats the system-owner deployment and usage figures supplied at the time of writing (for example 167 hospitals and more than 300,000 individuals impacted daily) as system-reported measures whose definitions and measurement dates require confirmation, and calculates no effect sizes. National-to-global roadmap

  1. 0
    Governance and discovery

    National steering group, facility inventory, legal and privacy review, baseline architecture and standards profile.

  2. 1
    Interoperability foundation

    National FHIR implementation guide, terminology services, registries, patient identity and matching policy, API security.

  3. 2
    Clinical and operational scale

    Core registration, encounter, diagnostics, medication, referral, discharge, inventory and patient-access workflows.

  4. 3
    AI validation and staged use

    Low-risk assistive features first; silent-mode validation, prospective evaluation and human oversight.

  5. 4
    Provincial and private-sector federation

    Additional hospitals, primary-care facilities, laboratories, pharmacies and approved private providers.

  6. 5
    National optimisation

    National surveillance, analytics, research access, resource planning and continuous AI evaluation.

Equity and access conditions include low-bandwidth operation, multilingual experiences, low-cost Android devices and measurement of adoption and outcomes by geography, facility type, language, age, sex and disability.

Contribution

The authors’ contribution in the manuscript is architecture analysis, standards mapping, workflow modelling and the development of a measurement framework. The paper includes workflow specifications for registration and identity resolution, clinical encounters, AI-assisted decision support, device integration, medication safety, discharge and referral, commodity traceability and ICD-11 surveillance, together with downtime and recovery management and an acceptance checklist.

Research integrity

Operational figures and capabilities come from system-owner materials and have not been independently audited. No patient data was analysed, and the paper does not assert diagnostic accuracy, mortality reduction, time or cost savings, or causal clinical benefit.
