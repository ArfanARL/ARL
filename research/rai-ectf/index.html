CONCEPTUAL FRAMEWORK · MANUSCRIPT

From AI Ethics Awareness to Measurable Ethical Competence

A conceptual framework — the Responsible AI Ethical Competence and Training Framework (RAI-ECTF) — for turning AI-ethics awareness into measurable, governance-connected competence for generative and agentic AI.

ResearchMuhammad Arfan

The question

AI-ethics training often stops at principles and awareness, leaving open whether the people who build, buy, supervise and use AI systems can recognise and act on ethical risk. Taking the Weidinger et al. taxonomy of 21 language-model risks in six areas as a baseline, the paper asks what changes for training and governance in light of later work on AI literacy, responsible-AI education, risk management, management-system standards and regulation.

Seven persistent gaps

  1. Risk categories are not translated into learner competencies.
  2. Performance-based assessment is scarce; awareness is measured by self-report.
  3. Training is detached from organisational governance controls.
  4. Lifecycle coverage is thin; training is event-based rather than continuous.
  5. Generative and agentic risks are underrepresented.
  6. Contextual and cultural adaptation is weak.
  7. Mechanisms for continuous development and reassessment are limited.

The RAI-ECTF framework

The Responsible AI Ethical Competence and Training Framework combines three competence dimensions — knowledge, judgment and action — with five progression levels and twelve competency domains, supported by a staged training architecture, role-based pathways and multi-method assessment. Its organising logic is Risk → Competence → Progression → Training → Assessment → Governance → Reassessment.

Progression levels

1Understand

Explain core AI concepts, capabilities, limitations, ethical principles and common risks.

2Apply

Use policies, safeguards and responsible-use procedures in routine situations.

3Evaluate

Analyse ambiguous cases, trade-offs, uncertainty and evidence; justify decisions.

4Govern

Translate ethical requirements into controls, documentation, oversight, procurement and escalation.

5Create

Design or redesign AI-enabled processes, training, safeguards or governance mechanisms proactively.

Twelve competency domains

C1AI foundations and limitations

Models, data, training and inference, uncertainty, hallucination and capability boundaries.

C2Human-centred AI

Human agency, oversight, accessibility, inclusion and impact on people.

C3Ethical reasoning

Principled reasoning, stakeholders, value conflicts, proportionality and justification.

C4Fairness, bias and inclusion

Data and model bias, disparate impacts, representational harms and inclusive evaluation.

C5Privacy and data ethics

Consent, minimisation, confidentiality, sensitive data, provenance and retention.

C6Reliability and epistemic integrity

Factuality, uncertainty, provenance, verification and source criticism.

C7AI security and misuse

Prompt injection, data leakage, abuse, fraud, cyber misuse and access control.

C8Human–AI interaction

Automation bias, overreliance, anthropomorphism, vulnerability and human factors.

C9Transparency and explainability

Disclosure, documentation, traceability, model and system documentation, and limitations.

C10AI governance and regulation

Roles, accountability, risk management, impact assessment, procurement and policy.

C11Socioeconomic and environmental responsibility

Labour effects, accessibility, distributional impacts, resource use and sustainability.

C12Responsible AI operations

Monitoring, incidents, change management, audit evidence, reassessment and improvement.

Assessment and governance

Evidence of competence is gathered through knowledge tests, scenario-based judgment, observed performance, portfolios and behavioural evidence — and connected to risk registers, impact assessments, incident reporting and oversight. The paper positions the framework against the NIST AI risk-management guidance, ISO/IEC 42001, UNESCO competency frameworks and the 2026 recalibration of the EU AI Act’s AI-literacy provision.

Contribution and limits

The contribution is conceptual: a complementary competence layer, not a replacement for ethical principles, risk frameworks or management standards. The review is purposive rather than exhaustive and the framework has not been empirically validated.
